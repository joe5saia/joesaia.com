One book, forty pens: the pecia system

Thirteenth-century universities had a throughput problem. Every student needed the same few very long books, and a professional scribe managed about one leaf a day. The fix was neither a faster scribe nor a new technology. The stationer kept one checked master copy as a pile of loose, numbered pieces and rented them out one at a time.

1 The bottleneck

Pick a book from the official Paris price lists of 1275 and 1304. A pecia in Paris was normally a gathering of four leaves (two folded sheets, eight pages, sixteen columns). The figures reported for a careful professional scribe come to about a leaf and a few lines per day, or roughly four days per piece.

Treat this as an order of magnitude: exemplar leaves and copy leaves were not the same size. The point stands, though. If the master copy is a bound book, one person holds it for most of a year, and every other student waits.

Scribes knew it, and said so. A stock line from colophons: Scribere qui nescit nullum putat esse laborem, “Whoever can’t write thinks writing is no work.”

2 The trick

The stationer (stationarius) was a bookseller sworn to the university. For each set text he kept an exemplar: a copy checked for correctness and left unbound, as a stack of independent, numbered pieces (peciae). A customer, or a scribe working for one, rented piece 1, copied it, returned it, rented piece 2, and so on. While one scribe worked on piece 7, others could be on pieces 3, 12 and 40. The fee was set by the university, not by the stationer.

Note what this buys and what it costs. It buys parallelism: as many scribes as there are pieces can work on one text at the same time. It buys fidelity: every copy is made directly from the same checked master, not from a copy of a copy. The cost is a shared single point of failure. Pieces wear, tear, go missing and get replaced, and whatever happens to piece 12 happens to every copy made from it afterwards.

Destrez thought the word pecia came from the parchment-makers: a “piece” was a prepared skin, folded into a gathering. So it is a tanners’ word that got promoted.

Hugues Shooner called it “a first attempt at industrial book production before the invention of printing.” The section below lets you check that claim.

3 Mind the shop

Paris, 1304. You're a sworn stationer, and on your shelf sits the official exemplar of Thomas Aquinas's commentary De sensu et sensato: ten loose pieces, listed at 8 pence for the whole set. Scribes will turn up and ask for the next piece they need. Hand it over, collect your pence, and keep them moving. Returned pieces land in the basket. Check them yourself, or let Colin the apprentice shelve them without looking. Every seventh day the bell rings and the peciarii come to inspect.

A pecia worn out by a hundred careless hands is a cheap thing to lend. Paying 10 shillings because a sharp-eyed friar denounced you is not.

    4 The model, minus the drama

    Same shop, no customers to charm: just the queueing. A small discrete-event model. Each scribe wants one complete copy. The shop serves requests first come, first served. Each loan takes a jittered number of days. Every loan has a small chance of damaging a piece. A damaged piece stays in circulation until someone notices: a scribe who complains on return, or, under Bologna-style rules, the weekly inspection by the peciarii. Repair takes the piece off the shelf for a few days and puts back a different text of that piece.

    5 Rules & prices

    This was regulated. In Paris the university made stationers swear an oath, repeated every year or two, and threatened to cut off anyone who broke it. In this case the regulator’s worry was not speed but correctness and price gouging.

    “…since many damages come from corrupt and faulty exemplars, we have decreed that the said booksellers swear that they will apply care and pains with all diligence and toil to have true and correct exemplars, and that for exemplars they shall not demand from anyone anything beyond the just and moderate rent… assessed by the university or its deputies.” University of Paris, statute on stationers, 8 December 1275 (Chartularium Universitatis Parisiensis I, pp. 532–534; tr. L. Thorndike)

    Bologna went further and created inspectors. Each year the rectors and councillors of the (student-run) university chose six peciarii, three from north of the Alps and three from Italy, with jurisdiction over “cases of peciae and corrupt texts.”

    “We will that a stationer, for each corrupt pecia which he gives out and for each offense, shall incur a fine of ten solidi Bolognese, and nonetheless he shall be required to reimburse the scholar at double. Half the penalty shall go to the university, half of the remaining half to the peciarii, the remainder to the denouncer.” Statutes of the University of Bologna, 1317–1347, rubric xix (ed. Malagola, 1888)

    The same rubric has the peciarii examine every stationer’s exemplars on feast days, have defective pieces corrected at the stationer’s expense, and meet at least weekly or pay a fine themselves. The denouncer’s quarter of the fine was a bounty for spotting errors. Tick “Bologna rules” in the simulator to see what weekly inspection does to the number of corrupt copies.

    Do the maths: one denounced piece costs 10 solidi, or 120 pence. The whole Catena on Mark rents for 17 pence in Paris. (Bolognese and Parisian shillings were different coins, so this tells you the scale, not an exchange rate.) Bologna was not joking.

    6 The evidence on the page, and you as the scribe

    No ledger from a Paris stationer survives. We know about the system mainly because scribes left notes to themselves. To keep track of which rented piece they had reached, they wrote a small mark in the margin where the new piece began (the usual Paris habit) or where the old one ended (common in Bologna). Then the binder trimmed the margins, and most of those notes went with the offcuts.

    7 What editors do with it

    The pecia system breaks the usual method of textual criticism. In ordinary copying, manuscripts form a family tree. Each copy inherits its parent’s mistakes and adds its own, so shared errors let you reconstruct the tree. Antoine Dondaine of the Leonine Commission, which edits Aquinas, argued in 1961 that the family-tree method yields nothing for a university tradition. Every manuscript hangs off the same exemplar, or a few of them, so the tree collapses into a star.

    Numbers in the circles count accumulated errors, assuming each act of copying adds about two. Illustrative only.

    What the star gives you instead is a history per piece. The Leonine edition of Aquinas’s De malo (vol. 23) describes an exemplar of 28 pieces, “each of these 28 peciae is a unit from the viewpoint of research inasmuch as each has its own history of deterioration, of correction, and of either total or partial replacement. For this reason we have worked strictly by pecia.” Its editors trace the earliest state of the Paris text, which they call α, and the later states it degraded into (β, γ). Earlier, H.-D. Saffrey had shown that several exemplars of one work could circulate at once and that their pieces were interchangeable. So a copy can change textual family at a piece boundary, in the middle of a sentence.

    That is the right-hand panel in the simulator. Each row is a finished manuscript and each column is a piece. With wear on and repairs happening, the columns change colour: copies made before the repair carry α, copies made after carry β. The “copies disagree” count tells you how many pieces an editor would have to treat as separate problems.

    One more honest point: a pecia text is not automatically a good one. The same Leonine preface notes that the exemplar inherited Aquinas’s own slips (“his handwriting is bad”) and any intermediate copying, and then “passed through hundreds of hands that left their traces on it.”

    Aquinas’s own handwriting is so hard to read that palaeographers call it littera inintelligibilis. Every exemplar of his works began as somebody’s brave attempt to read it.

    8 Where & when

    1. c. 1200Graham Pollard argues the system began at Bologna, the oldest university, around this time.
    2. 1228The commune of Vercelli, courting masters who were leaving Padua, promises two exemplatores with exemplars “correct in both text and gloss, so that the scholars may pay for their copies at a price set by the rectors.”
    3. c. 1250First signs in Paris, apparently inside the Dominican convent of Saint-Jacques, before the licensed stationers take the method up.
    4. 1264Padua’s statutes: cum absque exemplaribus universitas scholarium stare non possit, “since a university of scholars cannot stand without exemplars.”
    5. 1275Paris statute on stationers (8 December) and the first surviving Paris price list of 138 works.
    6. 1270–1350Shooner’s estimate of the system’s peak.
    7. 1304Second Paris list, 156 works.
    8. 1317–1347Bologna statutes with the six peciarii, fines, layout rules and a price list revised every ten years (121 legal texts).
    9. c. 1330–1350Paris use fades. The Hundred Years’ War (from 1337) and the Black Death (1347–48) finish it off. In the Italian universities exemplar copying continues until printing arrives.
    10. 1906The Leonine edition’s introduction to the Supplementum of the Summa gives the first solid study of copying by exemplar and pecia.
    11. 1935Jean Destrez publishes La pecia dans les manuscrits universitaires. He went through more than 7,000 manuscripts leaf by leaf, found nearly a thousand with piece marks and identified about thirty surviving exemplars.

    Pollard counted at least eleven universities where the system existed: Bologna, Padua, Vercelli, Perugia, Treviso and Florence in northern Italy, Naples, Salamanca, Paris, Toulouse, and Oxford. Destrez identified actual exemplars and copies only from Bologna, Paris, Oxford and Naples. For the other seven, the evidence is their statutes. No trace has been found at Salerno, Montpellier, Orléans, Angers, Avignon or Cambridge, or at any German or Dutch university.

    9 What we don’t know

    • The four-step picture is a model. Pollard’s “author → apograph → stationer’s pieces → pecia copy” is an ideal type. No evidence links an author directly to a stationer’s exemplar.
    • Oxford is disputed. Destrez and Pollard argued for an Oxford system. Parkes and de Hamel doubt it, and recent work (Ray 2015) suggests that most English pecia copies were made in Paris and carried home.
    • Piece size varied. Four leaves was the norm, but a Bologna quaternus could count as two peciae, and at least one exemplar mixes both kinds.
    • Order of copying. The simulator’s “any free piece” mode is a speculation. Pollard thought pieces were sometimes copied out of order, but the usual assumption is sequential renting.
    • Destrez’s book was a first sketch. The editors of the 1988 symposium volume called it “a preliminary overview,” and his card files are still being worked through (Murano 2005 catalogues the works known to have circulated this way).
    • The simulator invents its parameters. The rules are real (fixed fees, inspection, fines, repair) but the probabilities are not. No source gives a wear rate per loan.
