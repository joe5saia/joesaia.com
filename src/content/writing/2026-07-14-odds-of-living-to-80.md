[Timothy B. Lee recently pushed back](https://x.com/binarybits/status/2077018795445485743) on the claim that AI will have ten times the impact of the Industrial Revolution. His intuition is that people in 1945 already possessed much more than ten percent of the useful knowledge humanity will ever discover, at least when knowledge is weighted by its effect on living standards. Airplanes, refrigeration, antibiotics, electricity, and modern sanitation were already real. The set of discoveries that can transform a human life may be finite, and we may be well into it.

This feels like the right question, but I am not sure knowledge is the right unit. Intelligence is an input. A better human life is the output. The link between them depends on what is stopping us from making life better today.

Imagine an oracle with effectively infinite intelligence. It can answer any answerable scientific question and design any physically possible machine. It does not automatically get land, energy, factories, capital, political legitimacy, or the right to make choices for other people. What could it actually improve?

I want to use Maslow's hierarchy of needs as a loose filing system for the question. I do not mean that people satisfy their needs in a rigid order, or that Maslow is a production function for happiness. It is just a useful prompt: health, food, shelter, safety, belonging, esteem, autonomy, and meaning. Where is there still substantial headroom, and where is the bottleneck actually more intelligence?

## Start with a full life

The chart above is my first ruler. I am using the probability of reaching age 80 as a rough proxy for getting a full life. It is deliberately less ambitious than curing aging. It does not capture whether those years are healthy, happy, or fairly distributed, but it gives the question a hard edge.

For someone born in 1900, the probability of reaching 80 from birth was about 27%. For the 1940 cohort it was 52%. The Social Security Trustees' intermediate projection puts the 2020 cohort at about 72%. Conditional on already reaching age 60, the 1960 cohort's probability was about 68%.

Those numbers put an upper bound on the opportunity. Moving the 2020 cohort from 72% to certainty would be a 28 percentage-point improvement. Moving a 60-year-old in the 1960 cohort from 68% to certainty would be a 32-point improvement. Neither number is an estimate of what AI can deliver. It is the entire remaining space, including deaths no technology is likely to prevent.

There is clearly some reachable room inside that ceiling. Final CDC data put U.S. life expectancy at birth at [79.0 years in 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db548.htm). In the OECD's latest comparable profile, the United States was [2.7 years below the OECD average](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_15a55280-en/united-states_3517f35e-en.html), with materially higher preventable and treatable mortality. A [CDC study](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/ss/ss7302a1.htm) that defines premature death as death before 80 also finds large gaps among U.S. regions in deaths from heart disease, stroke, chronic respiratory disease, and unintentional injury. Its benchmark is what the best-performing states have already achieved, not a hypothetical medical breakthrough.

That distinction matters. Some health headroom is discovery-limited: better cancer treatments, earlier detection, new antibiotics, treatments for dementia, or a deeper understanding of aging. This is where much more intelligence could have enormous value. But some of the headroom is made of things we already know how to do: stop smoking, control blood pressure, prevent overdoses, make primary care available, get people to take their medication, and apply the standard of care consistently. AI can make each of those easier, but the missing ingredient is not primarily a scientific answer.

I am also tempted by the intuition that a healthy 60-year-old today often looks and functions much better than a 60-year-old did several generations ago. Comparisons of actors and athletes make this feel obvious. But celebrity photographs are a bad dataset, and I do not think there is evidence for a claim like "a healthy American is already at the 80th percentile of the human body's potential." Research on U.S. disability-free life expectancy is mixed: [we have gained years of life, but not clearly a larger share of life without disability](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4984740/). The honest answer is that we do not know how close we are to the biological frontier.

For this exercise, I am excluding the most speculative answer. If AI discovers how to make a 100-year-old physiologically resemble a healthy 30-year-old, this framework will badly understate its value. That would be wonderful. There is serious work in geroscience, but [the FDA's current summary is that no medication has been proven to slow or reverse aging](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/medication-health-fraud-specific-diseases-and-conditions). I have not found evidence that justifies putting radical rejuvenation into a baseline forecast. I am asking the narrower question: how much can we improve the odds of a healthy, active life through roughly 80?

That leaves three kinds of health headroom: catching up to outcomes that other places already achieve, discovering genuinely new treatments, and implementing what we already know more consistently. AI's leverage is different in each one.

## The bottleneck test

The easiest way to overstate AI's potential is to treat every unsolved outcome as an unsolved knowledge problem. I think each opportunity needs five questions:

1. **Discovery:** Do we lack the underlying scientific answer?
2. **Engineering:** Do we know the principle, but not how to make it reliable and affordable?
3. **Inputs and deployment:** Do we need energy, materials, capital, infrastructure, labor, or time at a scale we do not have?
4. **Institutions:** Do laws, permitting, incentives, coordination, or public legitimacy prevent deployment?
5. **Preferences:** Do people disagree about the outcome or the tradeoffs, or simply choose not to do what would maximize the measured result?

Infinite intelligence directly attacks the first two. It can help optimize the third. It can advise us on the fourth and clarify the fifth, but it cannot make political disagreement or conflicting values disappear. The realizable benefit is capped by the tightest constraint.

## Food is several different problems

"Better food" can mean avoiding hunger, improving nutrition, having excellent produce year-round, reducing environmental damage, or getting the experience of meat without raising and killing animals. These are not the same technological problem.

In a rich country, calorie availability is mostly a distribution and income problem. Locally grown strawberries in February are closer to an energy and engineering problem. Cultivated meat is still a biology and industrial-scale manufacturing problem.

Vertical farming already works well enough for some compact, fast-growing, high-value crops. The catch is that it replaces free sunlight with electricity and equipment. Current studies put indoor lettuce around [6 to 15 kilowatt-hours per kilogram](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-024-01045-3), depending on the system. Starting with the USDA's estimate of [148 pounds of fresh vegetables per person](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/112836), a deliberately rough calculation suggests that putting all of them indoors, while pretending every crop were as easy as lettuce, would require about 150 to 340 terawatt-hours per year. That is roughly 3% to 8% of [current U.S. electricity generation](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/state/unitedstates/).

Strawberries illustrate the harder case I had in mind. One 2025 indoor experiment produced about [10.6 grams of strawberries per kilowatt-hour](https://doi.org/10.5073/JABFQ.2025.098.001), or 94 kWh per kilogram. Applied naively to [recent U.S. fresh strawberry availability](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/107358/EIB-257.pdf), that would require roughly 100 terawatt-hours per year, another 2.3% of U.S. generation. At the [2025 U.S. industrial electricity price](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/prices-and-factors-affecting-prices.php), electricity alone would cost about $3.70 per pound before paying for the building, lights, HVAC, labor, pollination, packaging, or capital. At one cent per kilowatt-hour, the electricity cost would fall below 50 cents per pound.

So very cheap, clean energy really could change the set of foods that are economical to grow near the consumer. But it would not make indoor farming universally superior. Shipping a crop grown under sunlight can be far more efficient than converting sunlight into electricity, electricity into LED light, and LED light back into a plant. Greenhouses that retain sunlight are an important middle ground. The value of full indoor farming is freshness, resilience, land and water efficiency, and precise control, not energy efficiency.

Cultivated meat is less solved. Cheap electricity helps, but researchers still need better cell lines, inexpensive food-grade growth media, large bioreactors, contamination control, oxygen and heat transfer, texture, and factories. A promising 2024 study projected cultivated chicken at [$6.20 per pound in a theoretical 50,000-liter facility](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-024-01022-w), but the plant was modeled rather than demonstrated at that scale. A [recent review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39424999/) concludes that new bioreactor and facility designs and more work on cell differentiation are still needed before the process is broadly viable.

That makes cultivated meat a better candidate for a large AI contribution than vertical lettuce. There is still real biology and process engineering to discover. Even then, the winning product may be a hybrid that uses plants or fungi for bulk and cultivated cells for the fat and flavor. The goal is the human experience and the animal-welfare benefit, not reproducing a cow cell for cell.

## Energy is a multiplier, not a solved input

Energy abundance would expand the feasible set for food, water desalination, climate control, manufacturing, transportation, and computation. We already know how to generate much more electricity. I would stop short of saying we know how to provide arbitrarily cheap, clean, firm power everywhere.

The missing work is partly technical: storage, transmission, grid control, advanced geothermal, and perhaps safer and cheaper nuclear systems. It is also physical and institutional. At the end of 2024, about [1,400 gigawatts of proposed generation and 890 gigawatts of storage](https://emp.lbl.gov/publications/queued-2025-edition-characteristics) were waiting in U.S. interconnection queues. The median project that eventually entered service had spent more than four years in the queue. Not every queued project is viable, but this is not mainly a shortage of ideas for generating electricity. It is a shortage of transmission, equipment, capital, construction, siting, and agreement.

Infinite intelligence could design a better battery or reactor. That would be enormously valuable. If instead the cheapest available solar panel is already good enough, the task is to finance it, connect it, and persuade people to accept the wires and facilities. A perfect plan is not a power plant.

Housing has a similar shape. There is room for better materials, robotic construction, and more efficient heating and cooling. But in many high-cost U.S. cities, we already know how to build safe apartments. The scarce inputs are permission, land, infrastructure, and the willingness to let a neighborhood change. AI can make a building cheaper to design. It cannot legalize the building.

## Safety is not just a camera problem

Physical safety is the area where I am most wary of declaring the technology solved. It is true that ubiquitous cameras, facial recognition, better forensic tools, and targeted policing could increase the probability that an offender is identified. The certainty of detection matters for deterrence, and evidence supports focused interventions such as hot-spots policing.

But cameras are not a general solution to crime. A long-running systematic review found that CCTV has [a modest overall effect, concentrated in settings such as parking facilities and property crime](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/effects-of-closed-circuit-television-surveillance-on-crime/), rather than a broad effect on violent crime. Much violence occurs in homes or among people who know one another. Cameras do not remove addiction, impulsivity, illegal markets, domestic abuse, cybercrime, or the need for a competent investigation and court system.

Facial recognition also turns some technical errors into high-stakes institutional errors. NIST continues to find that false-positive and false-negative rates [vary by algorithm, image quality, age, sex, and race](https://pages.nist.gov/frvt/html/frvt_demographics.html). Better models can reduce those errors. They cannot decide how much surveillance a free society should tolerate, what evidence is sufficient for an arrest, who can search the database, or how abuse will be prevented.

This does not mean technology has little value. It means safety is jointly produced by technology, policing strategy, courts, social conditions, and public legitimacy. Infinite intelligence could improve prediction and deployment. It could not resolve the constitutional and moral tradeoff by calculation, because people value both safety and freedom from state observation.

Other forms of safety may be more technologically tractable than crime. Safer cars and industrial equipment, better weather and fire forecasts, early pandemic detection, and more resilient infrastructure have relatively clear objectives. AI can improve the design and the warning. The remaining question is whether a person, company, or government acts on it and replaces the physical infrastructure.

## The higher levels may be harder

AI looks extremely well matched to education and capability. A patient, personalized tutor can explain an idea ten ways, practice at exactly the right level, translate anything, and help a person turn an intention into a plan. There is still a lot of headroom here. The remaining constraints are access, motivation, credentials, and the fact that schools also provide peers, structure, and childcare.

Accessibility may be an even cleaner case. Transcription, speech generation, visual description, translation, adaptable interfaces, and better prosthetic control convert intelligence directly into agency for someone who is otherwise excluded. The hard parts are reliability, integration into everyday products, and making the tools available to everyone who needs them.

Mental health sits between medicine and belonging. AI could expand screening, treatment matching, between-session support, and clinician capacity. It may be a valuable complement. It is a much riskier substitute for crisis care, stable housing, financial security, or a trusted human relationship.

It may also improve lifetime utility by giving time back. Administrative work, forms, scheduling, shopping, basic legal navigation, and care coordination consume real pieces of a life. These gains are less dramatic than curing cancer, but they compound every week. They require less speculative science and may be among the most plausible large benefits from AI.

Belonging, status, and meaning are different. An AI can coach someone through a difficult conversation or help them find a community. It may provide companionship that some people genuinely value. But belonging is partly the knowledge that another free person knows you and chooses you. Status is positional: not everyone can be above average. Meaning often comes from responsibility, effort, and being needed. Infinite intelligence can create options around these needs, but it cannot simply manufacture their human content. It may even make them harder if abundance erodes work, agency, or the social roles from which people currently derive purpose.

## My provisional answer

There is still a lot of technological space to improve a human life. I do not think that implies there is unlimited space, or that every remaining improvement is waiting for more intelligence.

The health example is encouraging and sobering. Moving the probability of reaching 80 by even a few percentage points would represent millions of full lives across a large population. Some of that improvement could come from discoveries that AI accelerates. A meaningful share could come from making ordinary care cheaper, earlier, and more consistent. But the gaps already visible between U.S. regions and peer countries are evidence that knowledge is not always the binding constraint.

My guess is that infinite intelligence would have its largest direct effects in biology, materials, energy technology, personalized learning, and the elimination of cognitive drudgery. It would have weaker direct effects where the scarce resource is construction, institutional competence, political agreement, another person's affection, or a shared sense of purpose.

This is why the fraction of all useful knowledge we already possess does not settle the question. The last one percent of discoverable knowledge could contain a cure or an energy breakthrough with enormous value. On the other hand, knowing one hundred percent of what can be known would not guarantee that we build the transmission line, take the medication, permit the apartment, accept the surveillance system, or agree on what a good life is.

The real question is not only how intelligent AI becomes. It is where intelligence is still the bottleneck, and whether we can convert a better answer into a better life.
